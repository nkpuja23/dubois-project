The Tangent of the Veil

An interactive exploration of Viola B. Goin's 1933 letter to The Crisis, viewed through the theoretical frameworks of W.E.B. Du Bois.

The Archival Artifact

On October 16, 1933, Viola B. Goin, the first Black student at the Yale School of Nursing, wrote a letter to The Crisis. In this brief correspondence, she sought information on Black hospitals across the country.

This artifact serves as a tangible intersection of race, gender, and institutional access during the early 20th century. View Original Document in CREDO Archive

Double Consciousness & Ambition

Goin's letter perfectly encapsulates the "double consciousness" Du Bois theorizes in Of our Spiritual Strivings. She is operating within an elite, white-dominated space (Yale), yet her inquiry demonstrates a commitment to lifting the Veil for her own community by seeking Black medical institutions.

Defying the Compromise

In Of Mr. Booker T. Washington and Others, Du Bois fiercely critiques the limitation of Black education to mere industrial training. Goin’s pursuit of an advanced, specialized nursing degree at an Ivy League institution is a living manifestation of Du Bois’s demand for the higher education of Black professionals.

Navigating Systemic Supremacy

Goin's very presence at Yale, and her subsequent need to seek out independent Black hospitals, provides stark context to the environment detailed in The Souls of White Folk. The systemic exclusivity of white institutions necessitated the parallel development of Black professional networks, a reality Goin navigated daily.
